                                                                         BOUNDED GAPS BETWEEN PRIMES


                                                                                         JULIA STADLMANN


                                                   Abstract. Polymath 8b [5] proved that H1 = lim inf n→∞ (pn+1 −pn ) ≤ 246. In this paper we show how
                                                   the Bombieri-Vinogradov theorem can be combined with newer equidistribution estimates for smooth
                                                   moduli to obtain the improved bound H1 ≤ 240.




                                                                                          1. Introduction
arXiv:2608.31126v1 [math.NT] 31 Aug 2026




                                           In this paper we study H1 = lim inf n→∞ (pn+1 − pn ), the smallest gap between consecutive primes
                                           which appears infinitely often. The twin prime conjecture suggests that H1 = 2, but only since Zhang’s
                                           breakthrough [7] in 2013 is it known that H1 is finite. Zhang’s bound H1 ≤ 70000000 has since been
                                           improved several times. In particular, Maynard [3] showed that H1 ≤ 600, and the Polymath project [5]
                                           improved this to the current best known bound H1 ≤ 246. A core ingredient of Zhang’s result were
                                           his newly proved equidistribution estimates for primes in arithmetic progressions with smooth moduli.
                                           However, Maynard’s new sieve weights later allowed for the use of the older and simpler Bombieri-
                                           Vinogradov theorem [3]. Polymath’s bound H1 ≤ 246 also only uses the Bombieri-Vinogradov theorem
                                           and no newer equidistribution estimates [5].
                                           More generally, one may consider Hm = lim inf n→∞ (pn+m − pn ), the gap between m + 1 consecutive
                                           primes. Maynard [3] first showed that Hm is bounded, giving estimate Hm ≪ m3 exp(4m), and Tao
                                           obtained a similar bound around the same time. The Polymath project [5] improved this bound to Hm ≪
                                                     28
                                           exp((4 − 157 )m). Baker and Irving [1] first introduced Harman’s sieve into this problem, giving Hm ≪
                                           exp(3.815m), which we improved to Hm ≪ exp(3.8075m) [6]. Concrete upper bounds for H2 , . . . , H5 are
                                           also known, see [5] and [6]. The best known upper bounds on Hm for m ≥ 2 all use equidistribution
                                           estimates of Zhang type.
                                           Equidistribution estimates of Zhang type give an exponent of distribution larger than 21 , improving on
                                           the exponent in the Bombieri-Vinogradov theorem, but only apply to a limited choice of moduli, like xδ -
                                           smooth numbers. When applying the GPY method with Maynard-Tao sieve weights, a larger exponent
                                           of distribution corresponds to a bigger function support in the relevant optimization problem, whereas a
                                           restricted choice of moduli also restricts the support. In Polymath’s approach to proving upper bounds on
                                           Hm [5], the gains from having a larger exponent of distribution outweigh the losses from being restricted
                                           to xδ -smooth numbers when m ≥ 2, but not when m = 1.
                                           The aim of this paper is to combine the function support given by the Bombieri-Vinogradov theorem
                                           with that of newer equidistribution estimates, so that we may benefit from the advantages of both, and
                                           improve the bound on H1 . We prove the following:
                                           Theorem 1. The quantity H1 = lim inf n→∞ (pn+1 − pn ) satisfies Hm ≤ 240.
                                           The value 246 corresponds to the length of the shortest admissible 50-tuple, while 240 is the length of
                                           the shortest admissible 49-tuple. The bound H1 ≤ 240 is therefore the smallest possible improvement.
                                           However, while Polymath [5] used symmetric polynomials of degree ≤ 27 to achieve H1 ≤ 246, we only
                                           needed polynomials of degree ≤ 21 to get H1 ≤ 240. As such, we believe that our method could achieve
                                           bigger improvements, but due to limited resources and limited time, we have decided to present this
                                           modest improvement as a proof of concept.

                                           many tuples to which the available equidistribution estimates are applicable, but on the other hand it
                                           should be simple enough that we can efficiently integrate over the chosen region. Our basic idea is to

Having chosen a support and proved suitable equidistribution estimates, we need to solve the optimization
problem produced by the GPY method with Maynard-Tao sieve weights. This involves maximizing a
ratio of integrals, and can be transformed into an eigenvalue computation for a matrix whose entries
are determined by these integrals. Here one needs to choose a basis of functions to integrate. To prove
H1 ≤ 246, Polymath [5] used symmetric polynomials of the form ·p(t1 , . . . , tk )2 (1 − t1 − · · · − tk )b , where
p(t1 , . . . , tk ) is itself a symmetric polynomial of degree a and where 2a + b ≤ 27. To show the strength
of our method, we will use the same kind of basis to prove H1 ≤ 240, but with the stricter condition
2a + b ≤ 21. This means we get an improved result despite working with a significantly smaller basis.

However, trying to integrate symmetric polynomials over S = SBV ∪ SZ causes significant complications:
We need to obtain exact integrals as even minor errors in the matrix entries can quickly lead to incorrect
results. However, trying to use Mathematica to integrate a polynomial in 49 variables over a region like
this seems pretty hopeless: even for small degree polynomials and 10 variables a computation can already
take 10 minutes or more, and the duration appears to increase exponentially, so that we would never get
any result for 49 variables. Therefore we need to find a better way to integrate polynomials over S.

Some of the key ideas of our integral computation are as follows: We first consider sets

                                  Ts (k) = {(t1 , . . . , tk ) ∈ [0, δ]k : t1 + · · · + tk ≤ 1},
                                  Tb (k) = {(t1 , . . . , tk ) ∈ [δ, 1]k : t1 + · · · + tk ≤ 1}

and polynomials ta1 1 . . . takk (1 − t1 − · · · − tk )b . The integrals of ta1 1 . . . takk (1 − t1 − · · · − tk )b over Ts (k)
and Tb (k) can be expressed as polynomials in δ, with coefficients only dependent on the value of ⌊ 1δ ⌋.
To compute these coefficients we find a recursion which relates an integral over Ts (k) to integrals of
polynomials of equal or lower degree over Ts (k − 1) and Tb (k − 1). We then also integrate over more
complicated sets, like for instance Tm = {(t1 , . . . , tr , tr+1 , . . . , tk ) ∈ [δ, 1]r × [0, δ]k−r : t1 + · · · + tk ≤ 1}.
Such integrals can be expressed as matrix multiplications, using the coefficient vectors for Ts (k) and Tb (k)
produced in the previous step. The actual integral over S = SBV ∪ SZ is then split up into integrals over
scaled Tm , so that we can use the results of the previous steps.

Overall, our calculation of the needed matrix entries involves no integration at all, it only requires matrix
multiplication. However, many very large matrices need to be multiplied together, and so despite a lot of
effort to speed up our code, it can still take days to compute all necessary matrix entries for symmetric
polynomials of degree 2a + b ≤ 21. Our code also uses a lot of memory, a trade off from focusing on
computation speed. Due to these time and memory constraints, it was not practical for us to consider
symmetric polynomials of degree 2a + b ≤ 27. As such, we believe that with greater resources the same
method could be used to get a better bound on H1 .

1.2. Structure of the paper. In Section 2 we carry out the key steps of the GPY method with
Maynard-Tao sieve weights and Polymath’s epsilon-enlargement trick. Our arguments follow Polymath’s
approach [5], but many adjustments need to be made to deal with a more complicated function support
and account for the use of Harman’s sieve.
In Section 3 we record the equidistribution estimates used in our proof. Although the relevant estimates
are closely based on [4] and [6], the moduli restrictions in these papers are too strong, and we need to
prove relaxed conditions on the moduli.
The function support used in Section 2 is quite general and involves many different parameters. Dependent
on these parameters, the equidistribution estimates of Section 3 may or may not be good enough to cover
all relevant moduli. In Section 4 we show how to calculate good parameters for which the available
equidistribution estimates work well enough.
In Section 5 we briefly discuss how integrals over the chosen support can be expressed in terms of matrix
multiplication and recursion.
In Section 6 we combine the results of the previous sections to complete the proof of Theorem 1.

                                                 3. Equidistribution estimates
In this section we derive equidistribution estimates for various convolutions of coefficient sequences.
These will be needed to show that some minorant for the prime indicator function (denoted by ρ(n; x))
has equidistribution properties for moduli corresponding to Tk (δ, A, B, ε), provided that the entries of
B are chosen small enough: If the entries of B are small, then the corresponding moduli, given by set

5.2. Integration via recursion and matrix multiplication. To find eigenvectors of M2 M1 −1 , one
must of course first calculate the entries of matrices M1 and M2 . We need to obtain exact integrals as even
minor errors in the matrix entries quickly lead to incorrect results. However, trying to use Mathematica

polynomials in 10 variables a computation can already take 10 minutes or more, and the duration appears
to increase exponentially, so that we would never get any result for 49 variables. Therefore we need to
find a better way to integrate polynomials over Tk (δ, A, B, ε). We have carefully chosen the region to
make it possible to completely rewrite integration in terms of recursion and matrix multiplication. Below
